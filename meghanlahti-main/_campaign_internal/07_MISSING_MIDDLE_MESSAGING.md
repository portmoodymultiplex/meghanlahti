# Section 1 — The Messaging

This is what would actually appear on the Platform page, as a new subsection under Pillar 01: Housing & Growth.

### Headline / banner

> **More homes for the people we already love.**

### Subtitle

> Missing middle housing — a thoughtful path to keep Port Moody affordable for the families, seniors, and neighbours who built it.

### Body — paragraph 1

> Walk through any Port Moody neighbourhood and you'll find someone with a story like this. A retired teacher who's been here 35 years, in a four-bedroom house she can no longer manage, watching her grandkids grow up in Surrey because nothing smaller is available near her. A young family priced out of buying within the city they were raised in. A widow living alone in a house with a garden she can't tend, who would love a smaller home with a suite to rent. This is the affordability crisis, but it isn't a crisis of strangers. It's a crisis of the people we already love.

### Body — paragraph 2

> "Missing middle" is the kind of housing that used to fill the gap between a single-family home and an apartment building — duplexes, side-by-side townhomes, garden suites, courtyard homes, four-plexes that look like a single large house. They've been built across the Lower Mainland for over a century. Most North American cities have lost the ability to build them. Port Moody is one of those cities. Bringing them back, carefully and on lots where it makes sense, is how we give the next generation of Port Moody families a foothold here — and how we let people who built this community age in place, near the people and the places they love.

### Body — paragraph 3 (your voice / approach)

> The work has to be done carefully. Not every lot is the right lot. Design standards, parking, tree retention, and infrastructure capacity all matter. The goal isn't transformation. It's options — for the people who already live here, or who grew up here and want to come back.

### Pull quote

> *"This isn't about changing what Port Moody is. It's about making sure the people who made Port Moody what it is can still afford to live here."*
> — Meghan Lahti

### What missing middle actually looks like (visual feature)

A simple three-image grid on the page, with these captions:

**A garden suite (laneway home).** A small one- or two-bedroom cottage tucked behind an existing single-family home, sharing the lot. Often used for an aging parent, an adult child, or rented out to help a family afford the mortgage. Streetscape unchanged.

**A side-by-side duplex.** Two homes that share one wall, each with its own front door, yard, and driveway. From the street, it looks like a large single-family home — same trees, same scale. Two families call it home instead of one.

**A courtyard fourplex.** Four homes arranged around a small shared green space. Each has its own entrance and outdoor space. Common in older Vancouver neighbourhoods. Lower in profile than a typical apartment building, often less imposing than a McMansion.

### Addressing the four concerns we'll hear most

These can live as a small FAQ on the page, or be your talking points at doors and town halls.

**"This will turn our quiet street into apartment buildings."**
> No. Missing middle is the opposite of apartment buildings. We're talking about homes that look and feel like the houses already on your street — sometimes literally indistinguishable from a large single-family home. The point is to keep the streetscape, while letting more than one family live well on the same land.

**"There's no parking now. This will make it worse."**
> Off-street parking requirements stay. New homes have to bring their own parking. Homes near transit are the ones we'd consider first — those residents are the most likely to use SkyTrain rather than drive.

**"Our schools and sewers can't handle more people."**
> That's why nothing happens without infrastructure first. If a neighbourhood's capacity is at the limit, missing middle waits there until the capacity exists. Growth follows infrastructure, not the other way around.

**"Real estate investors will buy up everything."**
> Tools exist to limit this — owner-occupancy requirements, anti-flipping provisions, density bonuses tied to long-term ownership. We use them. Missing middle should help young families and aging seniors, not become an asset class for absentee owners.

# Section 2 — The Risks Worth Knowing

Two risks. Both manageable. Worth thinking about before we publish.

### Risk 1 — Political reality of densification in Port Moody

Densification is the third-rail issue in Port Moody. The residents most likely to vote are also the most resistant to change. The wording above is built to thread the needle — naming the opportunity, leading with empathy for people who can no longer afford to stay, conceding the legitimate concerns directly. But there's no version of this messaging that won't draw heat from the loudest 5% of the city.

The mitigation is what's already in Section 1: lead with seniors and growing families (sympathetic faces), use specific visual examples (concrete and small-scale), address concerns up front rather than waiting to be cornered. Don't lead the campaign launch with this — it's a Platform-page subsection, not the headline.

### Risk 2 — The Sheri / Dawar / Port Moody Multiplex connection

You know Dawar's working on Port Moody Multiplex, including the conversations he's had with city staff. So this isn't news between us. The strategic question for the campaign is: how do we get ahead of that connection publicly, before opponents do?

**My recommendation: lean fully in.**

I publicly own a "community advocate for missing middle housing" identity across all my channels (bio, socials, email signature) before missing middle launches as a campaign pillar. Then if/when anyone draws the Sheri/Dawar/Meghan line, the public answer is already in the open: *"Sheri's been a known public advocate on this issue, her family also works in housing, and she's been transparent about all of it from day one."*

That reframes me from "developer-adjacent campaign manager" to "housing-passion advocate with a known disclosure." It also gives you a clean, ready answer-line if a journalist asks: *"My campaign manager Sheri Zada has been a public advocate on missing middle housing, separately from her family's work in housing development. She's been transparent about all of that from day one. The policy positions on my platform are mine, shaped by 30 years of Port Moody public service."*

**The trade-offs of leaning in:**

- It puts a small spotlight on me. Once my bio publicly identifies me as an advocate, I become a person opponents try to engage. I'll need to be ready for that.
- It doesn't fully neutralize the connection. If you win and missing middle gets implemented, opponents will still try to use it. Lean-in reduces but doesn't eliminate the attack surface.
- It commits us. Once I'm publicly an advocate, walking it back later looks worse than never going public. So this is a one-way door.

**The alternative: play it smaller.**

We keep missing middle as a quieter piece of the housing platform, not its own pillar. My signature loses the "advocate" line. We avoid the connection becoming a public conversation. We accept that it's a latent risk that could surface, and we have a holding response ready if it does.

This is defensible. It gives the campaign simpler optics — at the cost of a less courageous housing platform.

## D. Sample commitments (draft — yours to rewrite)

For the website, under Meghan's Commitments. **These should reflect what you've actually voted for and are willing to defend.** Rewrite or replace freely.

- **Carefully, neighbourhood by neighbourhood.** Missing middle introduced where lot size, infrastructure, and street design support it — not as a blanket rezoning.
- **Strong design standards.** Setbacks, height limits, and design guidelines that protect the streetscape, the trees, and the character that makes Port Moody Port Moody.
- **Infrastructure first.** No upzoning without confirming sewage, water, traffic, and school capacity can handle what's added. Where capacity is short, growth waits.
- **Owner-occupier focus where possible.** Tools to favour homeowners over speculative investors — owner-occupancy requirements, bonuses for genuine multigenerational use, anti-flipping provisions.
- **Tree retention enforced.** Existing tree-protection bylaws strengthened, not weakened, as part of any missing-middle approval.
- **The province's mandate, shaped on our terms.** Bill 44 requires every BC municipality to allow small-scale multi-unit housing. Port Moody complies — *thoughtfully, on a Port Moody timeline, with Port Moody design.*
